Opening the Black Box: Explainable AI

An infographic that explains, for a non-technical audience, why large language models such as GPT, Claude, Gemini and LLaMA are hard to understand, how validation and performance metrics prove they are reliable, and what leading AI labs are doing to make them more transparent.

Introduction

Large language models are now used in hospitals, banks and law firms, yet even the people who build them cannot fully explain why a model gives a particular answer. A model can be accurate and still be a "black box." Before this project, I assumed that asking a model to explain its reasoning was enough to understand it. My research showed that this is not true: a model's own explanation can sound convincing without matching what actually drove its answer.

This infographic brings together the problem and the response to it. It shows why explainability is hard, how validation and metrics prove that a model works, and what the major AI labs are doing to look inside their models.

Description

The infographic is a single tall page with downloadable PDF and PNG versions. It is organized into four numbered sections:

  1. What Is Explainable AI? A plain-language definition and four examples of why transparency matters, in healthcare, finance, law and safety.
  2. Challenges in Explainability: four color-coded obstacles (scale and complexity, unfaithful explanations, bias and data quality, and regulation and access), each with a real piece of evidence.
  3. Validation and Performance Metrics: a four-step validation pipeline, a table of six metrics and the question each one answers, and a "trust stack" diagram adapted from the NIST AI Risk Management Framework.
  4. Current Solutions and Techniques: four approaches used by OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind and Meta, each tagged with the color of the challenge it addresses.

Objective

The goal was to explain the key obstacles to interpreting generative AI, describe how industry leaders are improving explainability, and show how validation and performance metrics support trust, in a form a manager or student without a machine learning background could follow. I also wanted to practice a different skill from my earlier artifacts: organizing abstract concepts into categories that reveal how they connect.

Process

  1. Research. I reviewed the course resources, then collected findings from primary sources: Anthropic's circuit tracing and chain-of-thought faithfulness research, OpenAI's sparse autoencoder work on GPT-4, Google DeepMind's Gemma Scope 2 release, a 2025 cross-lab position paper on chain-of-thought monitoring, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and the latest EU AI Act timeline.
  2. Classifying the challenges. I sorted the obstacles by where they come from: the model's internal structure, the explanations it produces, the data it learned from, and the rules and access around it. Sorting by source kept the categories from overlapping.
  3. Classifying the solutions. I grouped the techniques by which part of the system they make visible (internals, reasoning, outputs, or the model and its documentation) instead of by company, because several labs use the same approach.
  4. Linking the two. I gave each challenge one fixed color and tagged every solution with the colors of the challenges it addresses, so the relationships are visible at a glance.
  5. Design and build. I used a navy and white palette with one blue accent, Space Grotesk headings with Inter body text, and simple line icons, then built the page in HTML and CSS.
  6. Review. I checked every figure against its source, confirmed that text meets accessible contrast levels and that no information depends on color alone, and exported the page at 2× resolution.

Challenges and Solutions

The hardest part was explaining highly technical research, such as sparse autoencoders and attribution graphs, without losing accuracy. My solution was to describe what each technique lets people see rather than how it works mathematically: "look inside the model," "read the reasoning," "explain the output." A second challenge was keeping the content current. The EU AI Act timeline changed during 2026, when most high-risk rules were delayed to December 2027, so I verified the dates against recent legal analysis instead of relying on older summaries.

Value Proposition

This artifact shows that I can turn complex AI research into a clear message for decision-makers. Organizations deploying AI in regulated industries need people who understand both the technical limits of these models and how to communicate them. My background in tax technology, where every rule change must be tested and explained, connects directly to this need.

Unique Value

Most explainers either list XAI techniques or describe the problems, but not both. This infographic connects them, so a reader can see which solution addresses which challenge, and where gaps remain. It also places validation and metrics at the base of trust, making the point that an explanation is only useful when the model behind it has been proven reliable.

Relevance

Explainability is moving from a research topic to a legal and business requirement, with EU AI Act transparency rules applying from August 2026. This artifact builds on my earlier coursework on evaluation metrics such as precision, recall and F1, and on validation and red teaming, and it gives students, analysts and business leaders a grounded overview of where AI transparency stands today.

Key takeaway: trust in AI is earned in layers. Validation proves a model works, metrics show how well and for whom, and explainability shows why.
